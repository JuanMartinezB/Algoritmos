Abstract
Recent literature underscores the need for teachers to 
develop AI competencies with a recognition of the cur-
rent lack of well-defined competence frameworks. This 
critical review investigates teachers' Artificial Intelligence 
(AI) competence frameworks (AI CFTs), analysing their 
strengths, weaknesses and practical applications for re-
searchers, educators and policymakers. It identifies five 
distinct types of AI CFTs within Competence Construct 
Claims (Child, S., & Shaw, S. 2023). A conceptual approach 
to validating competence frameworks. Research Matters: 
A Cambridge University Press & Assessment publication, 35, 
27–40.), each addressing the complexities of AI in its early 
stages. Notably, frameworks derived from empirical data 
offer detailed descriptions of competencies, while those 
based on conceptual models provide broader overviews. 
Highlighting the need for further empirical research, this 
review helps identify and understand existing approaches 
to teacher AI competence development and paves the way 
for integrating AI CFTs into teacher education, ultimately 
enhancing educators' preparedness to harness AI in their 
teaching practices.

1  | INTRODUC TION
Artificial Intelligence (AI) has brought transformative changes in education, offering innovative tools and ap-
proaches that can enhance teaching and learning (Chakravorti, 2022; Gill et al., 2023; Grubaugh & Levitt, 2023). 
AI in education promises to support students with special needs, reduce school dropout, personalise learning, 
support decision-making, generate feedback, assess new skills, etc. (Bentley et al., 2018; Brundage, 2019; Vincent-
Lancrin & van der Vlies, 2020). Additionally, AI in education (AIED) brings a fresh set of competencies that edu-
cators should cultivate. The rapid advancement of AI technologies requires an understanding of AI capabilities 
and limitations. This entails more than just basic digital literacy; it encompasses data literacy, algorithm literacy, 
computational thinking and design thinking. Notably, the ethical use of AI in educational settings becomes a 
paramount concern, making AI and data ethics a crucial aspect of teacher competence (Berendt et al., 2020; U.S. 
Department of Education, Office of Educational Technology, 2023; European Commission, 2022).
Even as schools increasingly embrace AI tools, teacher preparation is not prioritised and is experiencing delays. 
A recent UNESCO global survey, which included over 450 schools and universities, revealed that fewer than 10% 
of them have established institutional policies or formal guidance regarding the use of generative AI applications 
(UNESCO survey, 2023).
The rise of AI in education has provided food for discussion on teachers' AI competencies in recent litera-
ture.2020 Trujillo-Cabezas (2020) proposed AI digital competencies for educators, including the utilisation of 
applications, adept information management, content creation for learning and facilitating student connections 
through technology. Teachers require both knowledge and tools to evaluate the appropriateness of AI-supported 
activities in achieving their educational objectives and transform their way of teaching (Flogie & Krabonja, 2023). 
Liu and Li (2022) defined teacher human capital for AI in education through three facets: expertise and proficiency 
in AI in education, emotional disposition and attitudes relevant to AI in education, and ethical considerations for 
using AI in education. Xu (2020) underscores educators developing AI competency for transformative roles, while 
Markauskaite et al. (2022) support integrating digital technologies and gaining hands-on AI experience.
Despite the growing interest in AI competencies, the existing literature highlights a significant gap in well-
defined frameworks for educators to acquire competencies essential for using AI to the benefit of their learners. 
Ng et al. (2021a) emphasise the limited exploration within current studies of enhancing teachers' AI digital com-
petency through teacher education programmes. Polak et al.  (2022) conclude that, while examining various AI 
frameworks, the development of AI competencies involves a broad spectrum of skills, knowledge and attitudes. 
While some are specific to AI, others are adapted versions of digital competencies or essential competencies 
irrespective of technology use (such as problem-solving and contextual consideration). Ng et al. (2022) reflect on 
three educational theories; Bloom's taxonomy to view cognition levels of AI literacy education, the TPACK model 
to understand how educators should learn to equip their students with AI literacy (Ng et al., 2021b), and the P21's 
Framework to identify the four types of digital competencies in the 21st century. AI digital competencies, as out-
lined by the P21's Framework for 21st Century Learning (National Research Council, 2012), recognise AI literacy 
as a vital skill within a comprehensive framework. This perspective emphasises its role in fostering non-technical, 
critical and complex literacies. Regardless of these early attempts to analyse the AI CFTs, the emergence of new AI 
frameworks highlights challenges in understanding their purpose and utilising and adapting them for professional 
development programmes.
Currently, there are several critical issues related to AI CFTs that have been not addressed by previous study 
of Ng et al. (2022) (see, Table 1). First, there might be a scattered landscape of various AI CFTs, making it difficult 
for educators, researchers and policymakers to find and compare them efficiently. Second, different AI CFTs 
might exhibit varying levels of quality and effectiveness in preparing educators to utilise AI. Third, there might 
be a need for a deeper understanding of how AI CFTs are designed and how they can practically be incorporated 
into teacher education programmes. Finally, educators might not feel adequately prepared to leverage AI in their 
teaching practices due to a limited understanding of relevant frameworks.
